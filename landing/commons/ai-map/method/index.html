How the map works

What we include, how we read evidence and rules, how community contributions work, and how often we update.

Scope

The map covers higher education: public and private universities, general programmes and business schools. It is organised around three groups of people: students, teachers and instructional design (programme coordinators, instructional designers, teaching and learning centres). The list of tasks starts from the OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 and from the research we cite.

We never rate a tool in the abstract. What we describe is a tool used for a task: the same product can fit one task and not another.

Four kinds of information, always labelled

Provider what a company declares about its product, from official pages, with the date we checked it.

Research results of studies, with design, sample, what was measured and what we actually read (full text or abstract only). Preprints are marked.

Law what a rule says, in plain language, with the reference. It is not legal advice.

Experience what people who used the tool for the task report. It is never mixed with research and never changes the evidence level.

Where Didaflow adds its own educational reading, it is labelled Didaflow reading.

How we read the evidence

We start from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, then studies with a comparison group in higher education. Studies in schools or outside education are used only as indirect evidence, and we say so. Studies funded or written by providers are included with the conflict of interest in view.

We keep apart what studies measured: performance while using AI, learning (what remains without it), transfer, retention, satisfaction, time saved, equity, integrity and wellbeing. Doing a task better with AI is not the same as learning.

Each task has one of five evidence levels: no rigorous evidence yet, indirect evidence only, direct but limited, direct with mixed results, direct with consistent results. When there is no rigorous study, the map says so plainly: no evidence does not mean no effect.

Rules

For each task we check the EU AI Act, the GDPR, Italian law and the providers’ own usage policies. Uses the law prohibits are marked in red before the tools; uses the AI Act treats as high-risk are marked in amber. These summaries are not legal advice: institutions should check with their legal office.

What people who use it say

Under each task you can say, in one click, whether your experience matches what the research says. Answers are shown only once there are at least ten, and they never change the evidence level. When practice and evidence disagree, the scientific committee reviews the entry.

On each analysis you can rate a tool for that task, describe your context and what you observed, and reply to others. Your post stays linked to your browser, so you can come back and add how it went. No email and no account are needed; your name is optional. Please do not include personal data about other people. Contributions are published under CC BY 4.0, and anyone can report one that breaks these rules.

You can also tell us when a task, a tool or a study is missing. We review every report in the monthly update.

Updates and review

Every month Didaflow proposes new studies, product changes and rules that may affect an entry. The scientific committee decides what to integrate. Each analysis shows three dates: when the product was checked, when the literature was searched, and when the entry was last reviewed.
